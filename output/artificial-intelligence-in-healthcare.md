Rubaiyat Hoossain Mondal, and Prajoy Podder Year: 2023 Abstract: Artificial intelligence (AI) models are increasingly finding applications in the field of medicine. Concerns have been raised about the explainability of the decision-making processes that are made by these AI models. This article presents a comprehensive analysis of the prevailing trends in XAI and their implications for healthcare. The literature search is conducted based on preferred reporting items for systematic reviews and meta-analysis (PRISMA) standards for relevant work published from January 2012 to February 2022. The review analyzes the prevailing reasons for the implementation of XAI in the healthcare field, including reasons for using AI for diagnosis and prognosis, medication discovery, population health, healthcare organization, and patient-facilitating apps. The paper also presents a comprehensive examination of XAI methodologies as well as an explanation of how trustworthy AI can be derived from describing patient characteristics, including electronic health records (EHRs). The article identifies a range of challenges in implementing AI in healthcare, including the need for transparency, demonstration of diagnostic accuracy, and the need for evidence-based decision-making. Finally, the article concludes that although XAI has shown promising progress in healthcare, significant challenges need to be addressed for this technology to be a reality. Key Findings: 1. The review identified three main challenges for XAI in healthcare: (1) insufficient data availability, (2) diverse data sources, and (3) lack of standardization. 2. The study found several limitations of current XAI research in the healthcare domain, including the lack of attention to explainable machine learning (XML) and the lack of consideration of patient-specific factors. 3. AI-assisted decision-making processes are often perceived as being transparent, with the ability to demonstrate diagnostic accuracy and patient outcomes. 4. The review identified several key methodologies for AI-based decision-making processes, including probabilistic reasoning, rule-based reasoning, and deep learning. 5. The review identified several challenges for using AI in healthcare, including the need for transparency, the need for evidence-based decision-making, and the need for the development of standardized XAI methodologies. 6. The review identified several limitations in the current state of AI-based decision-making processes in healthcare, including the lack of patient-specific factors. 3-5 key finding bullet points:

- XAI is a challenging domain for healthcare research due to the lack of data availability, diverse data sources, and the lack of standardization.
- Existing research in XAI is focused on XML and has limited attention to patient-specific factors.
- AI-based decision-making processes are perceived as transparent, with diagnostic accuracy and patient outcomes being the primary concerns.
- Several key methodologies for AI-based decision-making processes exist, including probabilistic reasoning, rule-based reasoning, and deep learning.
- Challenges for using AI in healthcare include the need for transparency, the need for evidence-based decision-making, and the need for standardized XAI methodologies.
- Challenges for AI-based decision-making processes in healthcare include the lack of patient-specific factors.

The Artiﬁcial Scientist: Logiciest, Emergentist, and Universalist Approaches to Artificial General Inteligentieles (AGI) is a research paper that attempts to define what constitutes an Artiﬁcial Scientist and evaluate their ability to synthesize and explain the phenomena. The paper presents a set of requirements for constructing and evaluating hypotheses, including the need for deﬁnition, inductive inference, deduction, explainability, interpretation, evaluation, and experimentation. The paper argues that an Artiﬁcial Scientist must possess certain qualities, including representation of hypothesized information using inductive inference, abduction of cause and effect from observed phenomena, communication of scientific concepts and intuitions, interpretation of evidence, and evaluation of hypotheses. The paper also presents a conceptual framework for evaluating and selecting future experiments, and discusses risks to consider in experimental design. The paper presents three key findings: (1) An Artiﬁcial Scientist must possess a means of judging the plausibility and potential profit of, and control over, hypotheses in a line of inquiries; (2) an Artiﬁcial Scientist should design experiments to evaluate hypotheses and select future experiments, and should be able to synthesize and explain the phenomena; and (3) the risk of choosing to select future experiments based solely on the expected utility of the experiment should be taken into consideration.
